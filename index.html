Part I

Strategic and technical brief

Who Everest is, what they can actually make, and where North 50 fits. Read this first.

TLDR

They own presses. We own the climate, the site and the credential. The die is the deal.

  1. They can make any aluminum shape you can draw. Three presses to 3,700 tons, profiles to 460 millimetres wide, their own billet, their own paint, their own PVC compound, engineers and die design in house. Almost nobody else our size can talk to will cut a custom die without a fight.
  2. They do not pultrude. No public evidence of any fibre reinforced polymer line. That matters, because pultruded fibreglass is the only common frame material whose thermal expansion actually matches glass. Ask whether they have looked at it. That question is worth more than any purchase order.
  3. Zone 8 is a movement problem before it is an insulation problem. Over the Fairbanks service range a three metre aluminum member moves about eight millimetres and the glass it holds moves about three. Everything downstream of that gap is a seal.
  4. Their glazing pockets are sized for European triple glazing. The certified build is a 54 millimetre unit at 14.25 millimetres of edge cover. Vacuum glass is a quarter of that thickness.
  5. Their certifications do not work here. Alaska code requires NFRC 100 and NFRC 200 ratings from an accredited lab. Passive House and CE marking do not produce that label, and an unlabelled product gets assigned a punitive default U factor.
  6. They are import exposed and it hurts. Since April 2026 Section 232 applies to full customs value. Albania gets no EU rate because Albania is not in the EU. Every route to relief runs through American metal.
  7. We have the one thing they cannot buy. A Zone 8 campus with a cold weather test facility, a tempering furnace and a trained crew, a mile from a paved road and an airport with daily jet service.

The gap that eats the seal

Aluminum overshoots glass by about five millimetres per three metre member across a Fairbanks year. Pultruded fibreglass lands within a millimetre of it. uPVC is off the chart, which is why their vinyl line is not the Alaska answer.

02

Do they pultrude?

No public evidence that they do. Their published capability set is aluminum extrusion, uPVC extrusion, forging and CNC machining. Nothing about fibre reinforced polymer anywhere on their site, in their EPDs, in their ASI filings or in their US marketing.

Why that is not a small question

Pultrusion is a different machine entirely. Continuous glass fibre roving off creels, through a resin bath or injection box, through a preformer, into a heated steel die where the resin cures, pulled by a continuous puller at roughly 0.3 to 1.5 metres per minute, then cut off. Window grade profile runs 60 to 75 percent fibre by weight. Different capex, different chemistry, different quality control.

But the adjacent competencies are all sitting in that building. They design and cut their own dies. They compound their own polymer. They have in house engineering and a published research group. If any extruder is going to add a pultrusion line for a cold climate product, a vertically integrated one with die making already in house is a better candidate than a fabricator.

Pultruded fibreglass is roughly five hundred times less conductive than aluminum and its expansion coefficient sits on top of glass. It is the only common frame material where the frame and the glazing move together. That is why Marvin, Milgard and the cold climate specialists went there, and it is why the question is worth asking out loud.

How to ask it

Not "do you pultrude," which is a spec question with a one word answer. Ask "have you evaluated pultrusion, and what would it take." That is a strategy question, and it belongs with the owner or the engineering lead, not the sales director.

If the answer is that they have thought about it, the relationship is much larger than a glazing package. If the answer is no, we have just told them where their market is going, which is itself worth something.

03

Zone 8 and the movement problem

IECC Climate Zone 8 is the coldest zone in the country, designated subarctic. It covers Interior Alaska, the Arctic Slope and the Yukon Kuskokwim Delta. Winter design temperatures run minus 40 to minus 60 Fahrenheit, and Fairbanks exceeds 13,000 heating degree days base 65.

The service range nobody designs for

The number that matters is not the winter low. It is the total swing an exterior member sees. A dark finished aluminum member in Interior Alaska runs from roughly minus 50 in January to roughly 160 on a still June afternoon in direct sun. That is a 210 degree Fahrenheit service range, about 117 kelvin.

Aluminum against glass gives roughly five millimetres of differential per three metre member. Pultruded fibreglass gives seven tenths of a millimetre. Seven times less. Over a six metre run those figures double.

What that means in practice

  • Setting blocks, edge clearance and gasket compliance all have to absorb the difference, every day, forever.
  • Curtain wall stack joints and slip connections are sized for temperate climates in most published systems. In Zone 8 the movement is roughly double what a European system is detailed for.
  • The metal itself is fine. Aluminum is face centred cubic and has no ductile to brittle transition. It gets stronger and tougher as it gets colder. There is no metallurgical problem at minus 60. The problem is entirely conductivity and movement.

The condensation line

At 70 degrees interior and 25 percent relative humidity, which is realistic in a Fairbanks winter because outdoor air is so dry, the dew point is about 33 degrees. With minus 50 outside, the interior frame surface has to hold roughly 83 degrees of a 120 degree gradient on the warm side or it frosts at the sightline. That is what a thermal break is actually being asked to do here, and it is a great deal more than it is asked to do in Tirana or Boston.

04

Why seals fail here

The conventional sealed unit

A sealed insulating glass unit is a fixed mass of gas in a fixed volume. Assembled at shop conditions near 70 degrees and one atmosphere, then taken to minus 50, the gas wants to contract. Holding volume constant, absolute temperature falls from about 294 kelvin to about 228, and internal pressure drops to roughly 77 percent of atmospheric. That is on the order of three pounds per square inch pulling the lites together.

In reality the panes deflect inward and relieve most of it. That deflection is the visible pillowing you see on cold days, and the residual load lands on the perimeter seal in peel. Run the same unit to a 160 degree exterior lite in summer and the load reverses and pushes out. Interior Alaska delivers a full annual cycle of roughly six psi of swing, plus thousands of smaller diurnal cycles, every year, for the service life of the building.

That cyclic peel loading is the mechanism that kills edge seals. Desiccant saturates, moisture enters, the unit fogs. Warm edge spacers reduce edge conduction but they do not remove the load.

The argument to have in your pocket

A vacuum unit has no gas to contract. It sits at near zero absolute pressure permanently, carrying a constant fourteen point seven psi through the pillar array. The load does not cycle with temperature.

Zone 8 does not just tolerate vacuum glazing. It is the climate where the physics most favours it, because the classic seal fatigue mechanism is eliminated rather than managed.

The failure mode that replaces it

Vacuum units have their own problem and it is worth knowing before someone else raises it. The two lites are rigidly coupled at the perimeter seal. When the outer lite sits at minus 50 and the inner sits near 55, that is a roughly 105 degree difference across a single assembly. The lites want to be different lengths, and since they cannot be, the unit bows.

  • Over a 1.5 metre lite that is around three quarters of a millimetre of differential length that has to be absorbed as curvature.
  • A rigid four side capture resists the bow and pushes the stress back into the edge seal. Compliant setting and gaskets let it move.
  • This is exactly the asymmetric thermal shock case that serious vacuum glass makers now test to, and it is the right thing to ask any frame supplier about.

Three more cold failure modes worth naming

  • Gasket compression set. EPDM stays serviceable to roughly minus 40. Silicone goes considerably lower. An air seal that stiffens in January is not an air seal, and Zone 8 airtightness targets run at or below 1 ACH50.
  • Structural silicone cure. Will not cure below roughly 40 degrees. Wet field glazing is off the table for most of the Alaska year, which points hard at shop glazed and unitized assemblies. Ask what they unitize and where.
  • Fastener bridging. A stainless screw through a multi chamber break is a point thermal short. In Zone 8 those show up as frost dots on the interior, and the client photographs them.
05

The glazing pocket

Their Passive House certificate is tied to a tested configuration: a 54 millimetre triple glazed unit at 14.25 millimetres of edge cover, with maximum dimensions held to the tested parameters. A certified window system is certified as a configuration, not as a family. Change the glass and you are outside the certificate until somebody re tests.

  • Thickness. A vacuum unit runs eight to twelve millimetres against a pocket built for fifty four. Either their bead range already spans it, or it needs a new bead, or it needs a new die. Find out which.
  • Edge cover. 14.25 millimetres is normal for European triple glazing. A vacuum unit's perimeter seal is a linear thermal bridge, and burying it deeper in the frame is how you keep the edge above dew point at forty below. Ask how deep they can go without a new profile.
  • Restraint. Given section 04, ask what clearance and gasket compliance the pocket allows, because over restraining a bowing unit is how you break it.
  • Weight. A vacuum unit is roughly a third lighter than triple glazing. Their lift and slide hardware is sized for heavy insulating units, so this is one place their systems are over specified in our favour.
Asking without giving anything away

Every one of these is a normal question from a glazing engineer evaluating a frame. None requires explaining anything about our own work, and none should be paired with the reason we are asking.

06

The certification gap

The least glamorous item in the brief and probably the most expensive. Everest's performance evidence is entirely European: Passive House Institute certification, CE marking under the EU Construction Products Regulation, EPDs through EPD International, and Uf values in W/m²K.

The Alaska Energy Code is explicit. U factors must be determined per NFRC 100, solar heat gain coefficient and visible transmittance per NFRC 200, by an accredited independent laboratory, labelled and certified by the manufacturer. Products lacking a labelled value are assigned a default from the code table, and those defaults are deliberately punitive.

  • Zone 8 prescriptive fenestration sits at U-0.30, and serious Alaska builders target U-0.22 or better.
  • Commercial fenestration also wants NAFS testing, AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440, for performance class and grade from an accredited lab.
  • Their US marketing says their systems have been tested in the United States for wind load, air and water infiltration, thermal and acoustic performance. Ask which systems, which labs, and for the reports. verify
  • Authority having jurisdiction is the municipality in Fairbanks and the state in unincorporated areas, which is worth confirming for any specific site.
Why this is leverage, not a problem

Somebody has to pay to get a cold climate system listed for North America. Whoever pays owns a moat, because the next competitor has to spend the same money against the same small market.

The clean split is that Everest funds the certification because it opens a continent, and North 50 holds the territory because we brought the market and the test site. That is the second meeting, not this one.

Why they are motivated right now

Section 232, as it actually stands

The regime changed on 2 April 2026, effective the 6th. Most published commentary still describes the old rules. Getting this right in the room is worth more than any other fact in this document.

  • Duties now apply to the entire customs value regardless of metal content. Several importers report the rate went down while the bill went up.
  • The UK, EU, Japan and South Korea keep reduced rates under their trade arrangements. Albania is not in the EU. Albanian origin profile gets no relief, and smelt and cast country reporting is mandatory.
  • The Commerce inclusions process was terminated and replaced by a rolling Commerce and USTR authority. Duty drawback is sharply restricted. Foreign trade zone entries require privileged foreign status.
  • Separately, the 2023 antidumping case that named fourteen countries never included Albania, and the ITC voted negative on 30 October 2024. Petitioners appealed. The older 2011 China order remains in force, which matters because Everest has imported through Chinese ports.

Their billet is smelted and cast in Albania. Every route to a lower rate runs through American metal.

What the relationship can actually be

The complementarity is unusually clean

North 50 installs glass and distributes vacuum glazing. It has a site, a climate, a tempering furnace in the plan, a cold weather test facility, a crew and a credential. What it does not have is a way to make the frame. Today the frame is bought from somebody else, on somebody else's terms, in somebody else's geometry.

Everest has three presses, die design in house, and a paint line. What it does not have is a cold climate product, a US certification, an Arctic reference or a northern workforce.

Neither side can build the other side's half quickly. That is the entire basis for the conversation, and it is worth naming plainly at some point on Monday.

The JAIC pitch

This is the strongest card in the deck and it is not a request for money. Raise it late, and raise it as an asset we own rather than a cause we support.

The gap in the market

North America is losing independent cold climate building science capacity at exactly the moment when every manufacturer needs cold climate validation to satisfy energy codes. The CCHRC and NREL Alaska campus was restructured in December 2025 and is under funding pressure. Fort Greely's Arctic Regions Test Center is a Defense asset, not a commercial service. There is a hole, and it is not a small one.

What the Joy Arctic Innovation Center actually is

  • The former Joy Elementary School in Fairbanks, roughly fifteen acres, reactivated as a civic and industrial campus. A nonprofit anchor with North 50 Building Systems as the for profit tenant.
  • Three structures: the plant with office, the cold weather test facility, and the round civic building.
  • A tempering furnace in the program. Vacuum glazing needs tempered glass, and tempering capacity in Interior Alaska is a genuine industrial asset, not a line item.
  • A twenty five page proposal with Kevin Tennant's signature went to the FNSB Assembly in July 2026.

Utqiaġvik and Prudhoe are colder. You cannot easily get a container to either one. Fairbanks is the most accessible extreme cold site in North America: minus 40 to minus 60 design temperatures, over 13,000 heating degree days, a paved road, a rail head and an airport with daily jet service. For a manufacturer who needs a mockup wall instrumented through a real winter, there is no better address on the continent.

The trade school angle

The frame that lands

A high performance system only performs if it is installed correctly. A misaligned joint, a fastener through the wrong chamber, a gasket seated at the wrong temperature, and the U factor is gone and the water is in. The callback and the warranty claim land on the manufacturer, not the installer.

Trained installers are a product quality problem, not a philanthropy problem. Say it exactly that way. Everest can either help build a workforce in the north or eat the callbacks when somebody else's crew hangs their system at thirty below.

What we already have

  • N50 Qualified already exists as our certification language. The credential is named. It needs a curriculum and a cohort, not a brand exercise.
  • Apprentice built site furniture is already a design principle of the Joy campus, so the training programme is already in the architecture rather than bolted on.
  • The cold weather test facility doubles as a teaching floor. Students learn on assemblies that are simultaneously producing test data for a paying customer. That is a rare and very fundable structure.

What already exists on their side of the industry

  • The Aluminum Extruders Council runs a Department of Labor registered apprenticeship. Phase one covered industrial maintenance technicians, phase two added extrusion press operators and die shop technicians, phase three moves to CNC, robotics and welding, delivered through community colleges and trade schools. Everest will need exactly those people in Ohio.
  • The AEC and the ET Foundation run an annual international student design competition with more than $15,500 in scholarships. A joint entry under both names is visible industry credit at almost no cost.
  • Alaska's TVEP supports technical and vocational training aligned to identified workforce demand. Note that TVEP funds flow to a statutory list of recipients, so the realistic route is partnering through an existing recipient rather than applying cold. verify route

Low friction first asks

  • Mis extruded profile, scrap and obsolete dies for students to cut, fasten and fail on.
  • A few sample units from the 70 000 series for install practice.
  • An hour of Acker's engineering team reviewing the curriculum.
  • A joint entry in the AEC and ET Foundation competition.

Do not open with a dollar figure. Every one of these is free to them and all four build the institutional trust that a test bay conversation later depends on.

  6. They have never worked north of sixty degrees. No public evidence of anything above about forty two. We can give them the reference project that opens every cold market in North America.
  7. Say nothing about what we are building. They have three presses, an in-house engineering team and a research group that publishes papers.

The pocket problem, drawn to scale

Everest's Passive House certificate is tied to a tested configuration: a 54 millimetre triple glazed unit at 14.25 millimetres of edge cover. Vacuum glass is a quarter of that thickness.

Their beads and pressure plates may already accept a range. If they do not, the fix is a new profile, and that is exactly the conversation worth having.

Ask the question. Do not explain why we are asking it.

02

The glazing pocket

This is the section that matters most and it is the one only Jason can run.

What the certificate actually says

The Passive House Institute lists EV HYBRID as an aluminum frame with a PVC thermal separation at 0.17 W/mK and a graphite EPS insert at 0.032 W/mK, tested with a 54 millimetre pane build up of 6/19/4/19/6 at 14.25 millimetres rebate depth, with maximum dimensions held to the tested parameters.

Every number in that sentence is a constraint. A certified window system is certified as a configuration, not as a family. Change the glass and you are outside the certificate until somebody re-tests.

What that means for vacuum glass

  • Thickness. A vacuum unit runs 8 to 12 millimetres against a pocket built for 54. Either their bead range already covers it, or it needs a new bead, or it needs a new die. Find out which.
  • Edge cover. 14.25 millimetres is normal for European triple glazing. A vacuum unit's perimeter seal is a linear thermal bridge, and burying it deeper in the frame is how you keep the edge above dew point at forty below. Ask how deep they can go without a new profile.
  • Differential bow. Panes rigidly sealed at the perimeter bow when the two lites sit at different temperatures. Interior at seventy and exterior at forty below is a delta of a hundred and ten degrees Fahrenheit. Ask what clearance and gasket compliance their pocket allows.
  • Setting and support. Point loading on a thin rigid unit, block placement, and whether the pump-out and getter location can be concealed within their sightline.
  • Weight goes down, not up. A vacuum unit is roughly a third lighter than triple glazing. Their lift and slide hardware is sized for heavy insulating units, so this is one place where their systems are over-specified in our favour.
How to ask without giving anything away

Every one of these is a normal question from a glazing engineer evaluating a frame system. None of them requires explaining our mounting approach, and none of them should be paired with the reason we are asking.

03

Cold physics they may not have thought about

Everest sells into Albania, Malta, the Balkans, Boston and Durham. Their systems are engineered for a climate that never sees the conditions on our site. Some of what follows works in their favour and some does not.

Works in their favour

  • Aluminum has no ductile to brittle transition. It gains strength as it gets colder. The frame metal itself is not the problem at forty below.
  • Their whole business is the thermal break, and in Climate Zone 8 that is the only part of the frame anyone cares about.

Works against them until proven otherwise

  • Gaskets. EPDM stays serviceable to roughly minus forty. Silicone goes considerably lower. Ask what compound their gaskets are and what the compression set is at low temperature, because an air seal that goes rigid in January is not an air seal.
  • Differential expansion. Aluminum moves about 23 microns per metre per kelvin, glass about 9. Over a long member through a hundred degree swing that difference is millimetres, and it has to go somewhere.
  • Field glazing. Structural silicone will not cure below roughly forty degrees Fahrenheit. In Interior Alaska that rules out wet field glazing for most of the year and points hard at shop glazed or unitized assemblies. Worth asking what they unitize and where.
  • Fasteners. Screw ports through a multi chamber break are a thermal short. Ask how their systems handle fastener bridging in a high performance assembly.
  • Finish. Powder coat and sublimated woodgrain are their strengths. High latitude sun is low angle and less punishing than the Sunbelt, but ice removal, abrasion and thermal cycling adhesion are fair questions. Anodizing is done through partners, not in house.
04

The certification gap

This is the least glamorous item in the brief and probably the most expensive one.

Everest's performance evidence is European. Passive House Institute certification, CE marking under the EU Construction Products Regulation, EPDs registered through EPD International, and Uf values in W/m²K. None of that satisfies a US building official.

  • US code compliance needs NFRC certified whole product U factor and solar heat gain coefficient, rated and labelled, in imperial units.
  • Commercial fenestration needs NAFS testing, AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440, with performance class and grade from an accredited lab.
  • Their US marketing claims their systems have been tested in the United States for wind load, air and water infiltration, thermal and acoustic performance. verify Ask which systems, which labs, and for the actual reports.
Why this is leverage rather than a problem

Somebody has to pay to get a cold climate system listed for North America. Whoever pays for that testing owns a moat, because the next competitor has to spend the same money.

The clean split is that Everest funds the certification because it opens a continent for them, and North 50 holds the territory because we brought the market. That is a conversation for the second meeting, not this one.

Tariffs as they actually stand

The rules changed on 6 April 2026 and most published commentary is still describing the old regime. Getting this right in the room is worth more than any other fact in this document.

CategoryRateApplied to
Aluminum articles, entirely or almost entirely metal50%Full customs value
Derivatives above 15% metal by weight25%Full customs value
Derivatives with US smelted and cast metal10%At 85% content threshold
Certain metal intensive equipment15%Transitional, through 2027
Russian primary aluminum content200%Any amount
  • Duties now apply to the entire customs value regardless of metal content. Several importers report that the rate went down while the bill went up.
  • The UK, EU, Japan and South Korea keep reduced rates under their trade arrangements. Albania is not in the EU. Albanian origin profile gets no relief, and smelt and cast country reporting is mandatory.
  • The Commerce inclusions process was terminated on 6 April 2026 and replaced by a rolling Commerce and USTR authority. Duty drawback is now sharply restricted and foreign trade zone entries require privileged foreign status.

Read that list back against Everest's business and the picture is clear. Their billet is smelted and cast in Albania. Every route to a lower rate runs through American metal, which is the entire reason Ohio exists on their whiteboard.
